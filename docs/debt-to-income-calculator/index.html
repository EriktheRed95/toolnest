What your debt-to-income ratio tells lenders

Your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio is the percentage of your gross monthly income that goes toward debt payments. It's one of the first numbers a mortgage lender checks, because it measures how much room you have in your budget to take on a new loan. The lower your DTI, the more comfortably you can handle payments — and the better the loan terms you'll be offered.

There are two versions, and lenders look at both:

  • Front-end ratio — housing payment ÷ gross monthly income. For a mortgage, "housing" means the full payment: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues. A figure near 28% or below is traditionally considered comfortable.
  • Back-end ratio — all monthly debt payments (housing plus cars, cards, student and personal loans, support) ÷ gross monthly income. This is the ratio lenders weigh most, and 43% is a common ceiling for qualifying.

How to lower your DTI

You can move the ratio two ways: reduce the top number or raise the bottom one. Paying off a car loan or a credit card balance removes that monthly payment entirely, which often helps more than people expect. Avoid taking on new debt (or even financing furniture) in the months before applying for a mortgage. And documenting additional income — a raise, a second job, or stable side income — lifts the denominator.

Estimate only. Lenders calculate DTI using verified income and the specific debts on your credit report, and individual loan programs set their own limits. Use this as a planning guide, not a guarantee of approval. This is not financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good debt-to-income ratio?
Lower is better. For a mortgage, a back-end DTI at or below 36% is considered strong, up to 43% is acceptable for many loans, and some programs allow higher with compensating factors. Below 36% gives you the most loan options and the best terms.
What's the difference between front-end and back-end DTI?
The front-end (or housing) ratio is just your housing payment divided by gross monthly income. The back-end ratio adds all your other monthly debt payments — car loans, credit cards, student loans — on top of housing. Lenders care most about the back-end ratio, but both matter.
Which debts count toward DTI?
Recurring monthly obligations: mortgage or rent, car payments, minimum credit card payments, student loans, personal loans, child support or alimony, and other installment debt. Everyday expenses like groceries, utilities, insurance, and streaming services are not counted.
Does DTI use gross or net income?
Gross income — your pay before taxes and deductions. Lenders use gross monthly income because it's standardized and verifiable from pay stubs and tax returns. If you only know your annual salary, divide it by 12.
