Cash-Out Refinance Calculator

Estimate your new loan amount, monthly payment, and loan-to-value when you refinance and pull cash out of your home's equity.

What a cash-out refinance does

A cash-out refinance replaces your current mortgage with a larger new loan and hands you the difference in cash. The new balance is your existing balance plus the cash you take plus closing costs (if you roll them in). Because you're borrowing more against the same home, your loan-to-value rises — and lenders usually cap it around 80% of the appraised value on a primary residence.

This calculator shows the new loan amount, the monthly principal-and-interest payment at your new rate and term, the resulting LTV, and the maximum cash you could pull while staying under the cap. People use cash-out refinances to consolidate higher-rate debt, fund renovations, or cover large expenses — but it converts home equity into debt and often resets your payoff clock.

  • Watch the LTV cap — exceeding it can mean a denial or a worse rate.
  • Compare total interest, not just the monthly payment, before extending your term.

Estimate only. Rates, LTV limits, and closing costs vary by lender, property type, and credit profile. This is not financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

How much cash can I take out of my home?
Most lenders cap a cash-out refinance at 80% loan-to-value on a primary residence, meaning your new loan can't exceed 80% of the home's appraised value. The cash available is that maximum loan minus your current balance and closing costs. Investment properties and second homes usually have lower limits.
What is loan-to-value (LTV)?
LTV is your loan amount divided by the home's value, as a percentage. A $240,000 loan on a $300,000 home is 80% LTV. Lenders use it to gauge risk — the more equity you keep, the lower your LTV and often the better your rate. Cash-out refinances raise your LTV because you're borrowing against equity.
Does a cash-out refinance reset my loan?
Yes. It replaces your existing mortgage with a brand-new loan, typically with a fresh term (often 30 years) and a new rate. That can lower your monthly payment if rates dropped, but extending the term can increase total interest even as it frees up cash today.
Are closing costs included?
They can be. Many borrowers roll closing costs into the new loan rather than paying out of pocket, which is why this calculator adds them to the new balance. That raises your loan amount and LTV, so factor them in when checking against the lender's cap.
