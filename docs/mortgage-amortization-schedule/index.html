How amortization works

An amortizing loan is repaid in equal monthly payments, but the split between interest and principal shifts over time. Each month, interest is charged on the remaining balance first; whatever is left of your payment reduces the principal. Because the balance is largest at the start, your earliest payments are mostly interest. As the balance shrinks, more of every payment goes to principal — which is why progress feels slow in the early years and accelerates later.

This is also why extra principal payments are so powerful early in the loan. Every extra dollar permanently removes future interest on that dollar for the rest of the term. The year-by-year table above groups the twelve monthly payments into annual totals so you can see the balance fall and the interest share decline, and the payoff estimate shows how much sooner you'd be debt-free if you add to each payment.

Frequently asked questions

What is an amortization schedule?
It's a table that shows how each payment on a fixed-rate loan is split between interest and principal over the life of the loan, and how the balance falls to zero. Early payments are mostly interest; later payments are mostly principal. This tool sums the 12 months in each year so you can see the trend at a glance.
How do extra principal payments help?
Adding money to your payment beyond the scheduled amount goes straight to the balance. Because interest is charged on the remaining balance, a smaller balance means less interest every month after, which both saves money and shortens the loan. The schedule recalculates the payoff time when you add an extra monthly amount.
How is the monthly payment calculated?
It uses the standard amortization formula: M = P × r(1+r)^n ÷ ((1+r)^n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12), and n is the number of months. The extra principal you enter is added on top of this fixed payment.
Is this schedule exact?
It's a close estimate for planning. Real loans may round payments, apply payments on specific dates, or include escrow for taxes and insurance. Confirm the exact numbers with your lender's amortization schedule.
