Analyzing a rental property deal

Before you buy a rental, the numbers have to work — and the headline number most investors live by is cash-on-cash return: your annual pre-tax cash flow divided by the actual cash you put into the deal. It answers the question that matters most to a leveraged investor: "for every dollar I sink in, how much comes back each year?"

Getting there means building the income statement from the top down:

  • Effective gross income — annual rent minus a vacancy allowance, because no property is rented 100% of the time.
  • Operating expenses — property taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, and HOA. By convention these exclude the mortgage.
  • Net operating income (NOI) — effective gross income minus operating expenses. Divide it by price and you get the cap rate, which describes the property regardless of how it's financed.
  • Cash flow — NOI minus the mortgage (debt service). Divide annual cash flow by your cash invested for the cash-on-cash return.

Cap rate vs. cash-on-cash — and BRRRR

Cap rate and cash-on-cash answer different questions. Cap rate ignores your loan and lets you compare properties apples-to-apples. Cash-on-cash reflects your leverage and your real out-of-pocket return. A deal financed with little money down can show a high cash-on-cash return even at a modest cap rate. That's the engine behind BRRRR (Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat): pull most of your cash back out in a refinance, and the small amount left in the deal makes the return on remaining capital look outstanding.

Estimate only. Real deals carry costs this model simplifies — capital expenditures (roof, HVAC), leasing fees, utilities, and taxes on income. Always verify rents, expenses, and financing with real quotes before committing. This is not financial or investment advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cash-on-cash return?
Many rental investors target a cash-on-cash return of 8%–12% or higher, though what's 'good' depends on your market, risk, and goals. It measures the annual pre-tax cash flow against the actual cash you put in, so it reflects the return on your real out-of-pocket investment — not the property's full value.
What's the difference between cash-on-cash return and cap rate?
Cap rate is net operating income (NOI) divided by the purchase price, ignoring financing — it describes the property itself. Cash-on-cash return divides your annual cash flow (after the mortgage) by the cash you invested, so it reflects your leveraged return. A property can have a modest cap rate but a strong cash-on-cash return when financed well.
What counts as operating expenses on a rental?
Recurring costs to run the property: property taxes, insurance, maintenance and repairs, property management, HOA dues, and a vacancy allowance. By convention, operating expenses exclude the mortgage payment (debt service) and capital improvements — those are handled separately.
How does this work for a BRRRR deal?
BRRRR (Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat) aims to recover most of your invested cash through a cash-out refinance, which shrinks the denominator in the cash-on-cash formula and can push returns very high.
