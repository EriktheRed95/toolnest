Mortgage Refinance Calculator

See if refinancing your mortgage is worth it. Compare your current loan to a new rate, find your monthly savings, break-even point, and lifetime interest difference.

Is refinancing your mortgage worth it?

Refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one — ideally at a lower rate, a shorter term, or both. The catch is that the new loan has its own closing costs, typically 2%–5% of the balance. So the real question isn't just "is the rate lower?" but "do my monthly savings repay those costs before I sell or move?" That tipping point is your break-even point, and it's the single most important number on this page.

Monthly savings vs. lifetime interest

There are two different ways a refinance can help, and they don't always agree:

  • Lower monthly payment — frees up cash flow now. But if you refinance a loan with 27 years left back into a fresh 30-year term, you re-stretch the schedule, which can increase total interest even at a lower rate.
  • Less total interest — usually comes from a shorter term (say 30-year into 15-year). The monthly payment often rises, but you pay the loan off faster and pay far less interest overall.

This calculator shows both so you can pick the trade-off that fits your goal — cash flow today, or the lowest lifetime cost.

Estimate only. Results use principal and interest and assume your full remaining balance is refinanced. Your actual rate, costs, taxes, and insurance depend on your lender, credit, and loan program. Always compare a written Loan Estimate before refinancing. This is not financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the break-even point on a refinance?
It's the number of months it takes for your monthly savings to repay the closing costs of the new loan. If closing costs are $4,000 and you save $200 a month, you break even in 20 months. If you plan to keep the home longer than the break-even period, refinancing usually makes sense.
How much does it cost to refinance?
Refinance closing costs commonly run about 2%–5% of the loan amount, covering the new loan's origination, appraisal, title, and recording fees. Some lenders offer 'no-cost' refinances by rolling fees into the rate or balance — useful to compare, since it changes the break-even math.
Does a lower rate always save money?
Not necessarily. A lower rate cuts your monthly payment, but if you restart a 30-year term you may pay more total interest over time even at a lower rate, because you're stretching the loan back out. This calculator shows both the monthly saving and the lifetime interest difference so you can see both effects.
Should I refinance to a shorter term?
Refinancing from a 30-year into a 15-year loan usually raises the monthly payment but can save a large amount of total interest and builds equity faster. Compare the new payment against your budget — the savings only help if you can comfortably afford the higher payment.
