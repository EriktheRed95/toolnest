Rent vs. Buy Calculator

Should you rent or buy a home? Compare the true cost of buying — mortgage, taxes, upkeep, and equity — against renting and investing the difference, over your time horizon.

Rent or buy? It mostly comes down to time

"Renting is throwing money away" is one of the most expensive myths in personal finance — and so is "buying always builds wealth." The honest answer depends on your numbers and, above all, how long you'll stay. Buying carries heavy upfront costs (down payment and closing) and heavy exit costs (agent commission and transfer taxes when you sell). Those only pay off if you own long enough for appreciation and paid-down principal to outweigh them.

How this calculator keeps it fair

A fair comparison can't just tally rent against a mortgage payment — that ignores the down payment the renter still has to invest, and the equity the buyer builds. This tool puts both people on equal footing:

  • The renter invests the money the buyer spends up front (down payment + closing costs), and keeps investing any month that renting costs less than owning.
  • The buyer builds equity through appreciation and principal payments, and invests any month that owning costs less than renting.
  • After your time horizon, it compares total net worth: the buyer's home equity (after selling costs) plus side investments, versus the renter's portfolio.

The break-even year it reports is the crossover point — own longer than that and buying wins; sell sooner and renting-and-investing wins.

The assumptions that matter most

Two inputs swing the result more than any others: home appreciation and your investment return. If you assume homes climb fast and markets crawl, buying looks unbeatable; flip those and renting dominates. Use conservative, realistic figures for both, and try a range — the goal is to understand the trade-off, not to manufacture the answer you were hoping for.

Estimate only. This model simplifies taxes (it doesn't include mortgage-interest or property-tax deductions, which vary by situation) and assumes steady rates of appreciation, rent growth, and returns. Real markets are bumpy. Treat it as a planning guide, not financial advice — talk to a professional about your specific situation.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to rent or buy a house?
It depends mostly on how long you'll stay. Buying has large upfront and selling costs, so the longer you own, the more appreciation and paid-down principal work in your favor. Over a short horizon, renting and investing your down payment often comes out ahead. This calculator finds which wins for your numbers and time frame.
How does this compare renting and buying fairly?
It puts both on equal footing. The renter invests the money the buyer would have spent up front (down payment plus closing costs), and whenever one option costs less per month, that surplus is invested too. After your time horizon it compares each person's total net worth — the buyer's home equity plus side investments versus the renter's investment portfolio.
What's the break-even point for buying?
It's the number of years you must own before buying beats renting, given your costs, appreciation, and investment return. Below the break-even, renting and investing wins; above it, owning pulls ahead as equity and appreciation outgrow the upfront and selling costs.
Does buying always build wealth?
Not automatically. Owning builds equity, but mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and transaction costs are real expenses. If home prices stagnate or you sell quickly, renting and investing the difference can leave you with more. The assumptions you enter — especially appreciation and investment return — drive the result.
