Fix and Flip Calculator

Run the numbers on a house flip: maximum allowable offer using the 70% rule, total project cost, projected profit, and return on investment from ARV, rehab, and holding costs.

Does this flip pencil out?

House flipping lives and dies on the numbers, and they all start from one figure: the after-repair value (ARV) — what the home will sell for once it's fixed up, based on comparable sales. From there, experienced flippers screen deals with the 70% rule: never pay more than 70% of ARV minus the rehab cost. That 30% gap is the cushion meant to swallow holding costs, financing, selling costs, and your profit.

The rule is a fast first filter, not the whole story. This calculator does both jobs: it shows the maximum allowable offer the 70% rule suggests, then runs a full deal breakdown at your actual purchase price so you can see the projected profit and return on cash invested.

The costs that eat flip profits

New flippers consistently underestimate everything that isn't the purchase or the rehab:

  • Holding costs — loan interest, property taxes, insurance, and utilities for every month you own it. Flips that drag on are profit killers.
  • Financing costs — hard-money lenders charge points and high rates; private money has its own terms. Build these in from day one.
  • Selling costs — agent commission, closing costs, and staging, typically 7%–9% of the sale price.

Add them up and they routinely reach 15%–20% of ARV — which is exactly the buffer the 70% rule reserves. Run a deal where your purchase price creeps above the max offer and you'll watch the projected profit shrink in real time.

Estimate only. The biggest risk in any flip is an over-optimistic ARV or rehab budget. Confirm comps with a local agent and get contractor bids before you commit. This tool is for planning and is not financial or investment advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 70% rule in house flipping?
The 70% rule says an investor should pay no more than 70% of a property's after-repair value (ARV) minus the cost of repairs. So Max Offer = ARV × 0.70 − repair costs. The 30% buffer is meant to cover holding costs, financing, selling costs, and your profit. It's a quick screen, not a substitute for a full deal analysis.
What is ARV?
ARV stands for After-Repair Value — what the property will be worth once renovations are complete, based on comparable sales of similar finished homes nearby. Every flip number flows from the ARV, so getting it right (ideally with an agent's comps) is the most important part of analyzing a flip.
What costs do flippers forget?
Beyond the purchase and rehab, the big ones are holding costs (loan interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities while you own it), financing/points on a hard-money loan, and selling costs (agent commission, closing, staging). These can easily total 15%–20% of ARV and are exactly what the 70% rule's buffer is designed to absorb.
What profit should I aim for on a flip?
Many flippers target a profit of at least 10%–15% of ARV, or a minimum dollar amount that justifies the risk and effort. Thin margins are dangerous because rehab overruns and slow sales eat into them fast. Use this calculator to see how your projected profit compares before you make an offer.
