What price per square foot tells you

Price per square foot is the real estate world's favorite shorthand. Divide a home's price by its finished square footage and you get a single number that lets you compare a 1,500 sq ft cottage against a 3,000 sq ft colonial on a roughly common footing. Buyers use it to sanity-check a list price, sellers use it to position against recent sales, and appraisers lean on it as one input among several.

The second section flips the math: pick a price per square foot from comparable sales nearby and multiply by a home's size to ballpark its value. It's a fast way to translate "homes around here go for about $210 a foot" into a dollar figure before you dig deeper.

  • Smaller homes usually carry a higher price per square foot than larger ones nearby.
  • Use consistent square footage — finished, above-grade area — when you compare.
  • Treat the result as a starting point, not an appraisal.

Estimate only. Price per square foot varies widely with condition, updates, lot size, layout, and location — two identical-sized homes can be worth very different amounts. Use comparable sales and a professional opinion of value before making decisions. This is not financial or real estate advice.

Frequently asked questions

How is price per square foot calculated?
Divide the total price by the finished square footage: a $400,000 home with 2,000 sq ft costs $200 per square foot. It's a quick way to normalize price across homes of different sizes, but it ignores condition, layout, lot size, and location quality.
Is a lower price per square foot always better?
Not necessarily. A low price per square foot can signal a good value, but it can also reflect deferred maintenance, an inferior location, a poor layout, or a large home where extra space adds little usable value. Always look at the whole picture, not just the ratio.
What square footage should I use?
Use finished, above-grade living area for the cleanest comparison, since that's how most listings and appraisers measure it. Basements, garages, and unfinished space are usually counted separately or excluded, so comparing on a consistent basis matters.
Why do homes on the same street have different prices per square foot?
Condition, updates, views, lot size, and even the month they sold all move the number. Smaller homes often carry a higher price per square foot than larger ones nearby, because the value of land and fixed features is spread over less space.
